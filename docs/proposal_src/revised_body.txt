1. Introduction
Most of our own classes at the University of Education can now be taught online, and the same
is true across Pakistan and much of the world. A lecturer opens Zoom, Google Meet or
Microsoft Teams and forty, fifty, sometimes sixty students join from their homes. These tools
were built for business meetings. They carry the lecturer’s voice and slides outward and bring a
grid of small video tiles back, and that is where their understanding ends. Nothing in them
knows whether the person behind a tile is awake.
A teacher in a physical room reads the class without thinking about it. Someone nodding off in
the back row, someone staring out of the window, someone typing under the desk: each is
noticed within seconds and answered with a question, a pause or a change of pace. Online, that
loop is cut. The lecturer is talking, driving the slides and glancing at the chat, while the students
are a crowded wall of thumbnails that nobody can really read. A student can fall asleep, walk
away, stare at a second monitor or switch to a game, and the first sign the lecturer gets is a poor
quiz result some weeks later.
We propose to put that missing feedback back, with a system we call IntelliClass AI. It is a
complete web classroom, not a plug-in for somebody else’s platform: the instructor creates a
class, uploads the slides and teaches over live audio and video, and students sign in with their
own accounts and roll numbers. On top of that, and only after a student has explicitly agreed,
the student’s own webcam and shared screen are analysed. Vision models estimate whether a
face is present, whether the eyes are open, whether the student looks drowsy, and roughly where
the head and gaze are pointing. A second pipeline reads what is on the shared screen with OCR
and text embeddings and asks one plain question: does this still look like the slide being taught
right now?
Two decisions run through the whole design. The first is that the face analysis happens inside
the student’s own browser. Raw video is never uploaded, never stored, never even seen by our
server; what travels is a few bytes of numbers each second. That is good for privacy, and it also
means the server can carry a large class on free hosting because it is not decoding anyone’s
camera. The second decision is that no judgement is ever made from a single frame. An alert
engine weighs how confident the model is against how long a condition has lasted, then applies
cooldown and grouping rules on top. A blink, or a two-second look down at a notebook,
produces nothing. What the instructor receives is a short, ranked list of alerts worth acting on
rather than a stream of noise.
The system keeps helping after the alert, too. Lecture audio is transcribed with an open-source
speech model; summaries, key topics and likely exam questions are generated from the
transcript; and a retrieval-augmented assistant answers students’ questions from that lecture’s
slides and transcript only, saying plainly when the answer is not in the material. Instructors
watch a live dashboard while they teach and get an analytics report once the class ends.
In practice the project brings computer vision, machine learning and deep learning, natural
language processing, generative AI and a real-time distributed back end together in one
working product. We will judge every part of it with numbers: precision, recall and F1-score for
the models, alert latency, resource use, and behaviour with up to one hundred students
connected at once.

2. Related Work
Four areas of existing work matter here: the platforms used for online classes, research on
recognising student engagement automatically, work on drowsiness and gaze estimation, and
the tools now available for understanding lecture content. We take each in turn and close with
the gap we intend to fill.
2.1 Online Meeting and Classroom Platforms
Zoom [1], Google Meet [2] and Microsoft Teams [3] carry most online teaching today. All
three offer dependable audio and video, screen sharing, chat and recording, and some add
attendance logs or simple participation counts such as time joined or messages posted. What
none of them attempts is to tell whether a student is awake, in front of the camera, or looking at
the lecture at all. Spotting disengagement is left to the instructor, and that is precisely the job
that stops being possible once a class grows past a couple of dozen tiles.
2.2 Online Examination Proctoring Systems
Proctoring products such as Proctorio [4] watch a candidate’s webcam and screen for the length
of an exam and flag things like an absent face, a second person in the room or a switch to
another window. They prove that these signals can be processed automatically. Their purpose,
though, is almost the opposite of ours. They exist for short, high-stakes exams, not for everyday
lectures; they record the whole session; and what they produce is an accusation. Coghlan,
Miller and Paterson (2021) [5] argue that this kind of opaque, heavily recorded surveillance
wears away the trust between students and their institution. We have taken the other road: alerts
are supportive, nothing is recorded, and the system never labels anyone dishonest.
2.3 Automatic Engagement Recognition Research
Recognising engagement from a student’s face is a reasonably mature research problem.
Whitehill et al. (2014) [6] showed a decade ago that classifiers trained on facial expressions
could judge engagement about as consistently as human labellers, which settled the question of
whether the task is possible at all. Gupta et al. (2016) [7] then released DAiSEE, roughly nine
thousand short clips of 112 people watching educational video on their own devices, each clip
rated for boredom, confusion, engagement and frustration. Kaur et al. (2018) [8] went a step
further by estimating engagement intensity and locating where in an unconstrained video it
changes. The review by Dewan, Murshed and Lin (2019) [9] is the most useful summary of the
field for our purposes, because two of its findings are the motivation for this project: almost all
systems are evaluated offline on recorded data, and very few have ever been wired into a
teaching platform that an instructor could use during an actual lecture.
2.4 Drowsiness Detection
Most drowsiness research comes from driver monitoring. Soukupová and Čech (2016) [10]
proposed the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR), a single number computed from six landmarks around
each eye that drops sharply when the eyelid closes, and showed that it can detect blinks in real
time on an ordinary computer. EAR is usually paired with PERCLOS, the share of time the eyes
spend closed, and with blink rate; together they form the baseline that learned models are
measured against. Public datasets such as the MRL Eye Dataset, Closed Eyes in the Wild and
YawDD exist for training. We use EAR as our baseline as well, but the setting is different from
a car: the camera is a laptop webcam at desk height, the lighting is whatever the student’s room
happens to have, and the observation runs for an hour or more.
2.5 Gaze Estimation and Facial Landmark Tracking
Zhang et al. (2015) [11] built MPIIGaze by collecting more than two hundred thousand face
images from fifteen people on their own laptops over several months, and used it to show that
appearance-based gaze estimation holds up under everyday lighting and head movement, while
also documenting how it degrades at extreme angles and in poor light. MediaPipe (Lugaresi et
al., 2019) [12] and its Face Mesh model (Kartynnik et al., 2019) [13] then made it practical to
track 468 facial landmarks in real time on laptops, phones and, importantly for us, inside a web
browser. Together these results make on-device attention estimation realistic. They also carry a
warning that we have built into the design: when the image is poor, an honest system should
report an uncertain state rather than guess.
2.6 Lecture-Content and Document Understanding
The pieces needed to understand what is on a screen are now mature and open source. PP-OCR
(Du et al., 2020) [14] recognises text with models only a few megabytes in size. CLIP (Radford
et al., 2021) [15] maps images and text into one embedding space, and Sentence-BERT
(Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) [16] gives fast semantic similarity between passages of text.
Whisper (Radford et al., 2023) [17], trained on roughly 680,000 hours of multilingual audio,
handles speech-to-text in noisy conditions, and retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al.,
2020) [18] lets a language model answer from a fixed set of documents instead of from
memory. Each of these has been used many times on its own. We have not found published
work that combines them to compare a student’s live screen with the slide the instructor is
presenting at that moment.
2.7 Classroom Automation Systems
A separate family of systems automates the administration of a physical classroom. Chen et al.
(2020) [19] review the rapid growth of artificial intelligence in education and observe that most
deployed systems serve administration, and only a few look at learning as it happens minute by
minute. The typical project mounts one camera in the room, adds a mobile or desktop app, and
automates attendance by face recognition, quiz creation and hosting, record keeping and note
preparation, sometimes with an engagement report built from a photo taken every few minutes.
Useful as they are, these systems assume everyone is in one room under one camera. They
cannot follow an individual’s eye state continuously, and they have no view of what is on each
student’s own screen. Their reports arrive periodically and serve administration; our interest is
in what happens second by second during a remote lecture, and in reacting while it is still going
on.
2.8 Research Gap
Put side by side, the work above leaves a visible hole:
 • Meeting platforms deliver the class but learn nothing about the students in it, while
 proctoring tools read the same signals only for exams and only with full recording.
 • Engagement research is accurate but offline and tied to datasets; drowsiness and gaze
 methods are mature but tuned for drivers, not learners.
 • Classroom automation serves physical rooms and administrative chores, not individual
 remote students.
 • None of the reviewed systems compares a student’s live screen with the current slide,
 and none fuses these signals through confidence- and persistence-based alerting.

3. Problem Description
A lecturer running a large online class is asked to do two things at once: teach, and judge
whether dozens of remote students are still with the lecture. With every student reduced to a
small tile, the second task is close to impossible. Students doze, step away from the camera,
stare elsewhere for minutes, or drift to unrelated content on their own screens, and because the
lecturer is busy presenting, these lapses surface only when assessment results come back poor.
The tools that exist each cover a fragment. Meeting platforms give communication and no sense
of attention. Proctoring software does watch students, but it is built for exams, keeps full video
and raises real privacy concerns. Engagement research produces accurate models that are tested
offline on recordings. Classroom automation targets physical rooms and tasks such as
attendance and quizzes. A naive automated fix would introduce problems of its own: deciding
from single frames floods the instructor with false alarms, streaming every camera to a server
neither scales nor respects privacy, and poor lighting or an odd camera angle makes any
prediction shaky.
What is needed is a virtual classroom that works in real time and respects privacy; that turns
permitted camera, screen and audio signals into a small number of trustworthy, ranked alerts
while the lecture is still running; that keeps working when some signals are missing or
unreliable; and that is there to support learning, not to accuse anyone.

4. Aims and Objectives
Aim
 • To build an easy-to-use online classroom that quietly notices when a student has
 become sleepy, distracted or has drifted away from the lecture, tells the teacher only
 when it actually matters, and turns every lecture into notes and answers students can
 revise from, without recording or storing any video at all.
Objectives
 1. Build the classroom itself. A website where a teacher starts a class and students join
 with their roll number, see and hear the teacher, and follow the slides as they are taught.
 2. Notice sleepy students. Use the student’s own camera to tell when the eyes stay shut
 too long, and report drowsiness only once it has gone on for several seconds. Target:
 correct in at least 85 out of 100 test cases.
 3. Notice when attention drifts. Detect a student who has looked away from the lecture
 for a long stretch or has left the camera, and say “not sure” honestly when the light or the
 camera angle makes the call unsafe.
 4. Check that the student is on the right screen. With the student’s permission, compare
 what is open on the screen against the slide being taught, and report a mismatch only if it
 outlasts a short grace period.
 5. Send few alerts, and make them count. Never alert on a blink or a two-second glance,
 and group repeated problems, so the teacher sees a short list and not a flood. Target: an
 alert reaches the teacher within 3 seconds.
 6. Let the teacher reach one student privately. A tap on an alert plays a soft sound and
 shows a short message on that student’s device alone, so nobody else is disturbed and
 nobody is embarrassed in front of the class.
 7. Give the teacher a live view and a report afterwards. Show who needs attention right
 now during the lecture, and build a summary report automatically once it ends.
 8. Report with signals, never pictures. The teacher receives only short status words per
 student, such as sleepy, camera off, no face in front of the camera, looking away, wrong
 screen or connection problem. No video, photograph or screenshot is ever sent, so the
 teacher sees at a glance who needs attention while no image of any student leaves that
 student’s own device.
 9. Give each student a private summary of their own. Every student can see their own
 attention and participation record and nobody else’s, so the system helps them improve
 instead of merely reporting on them.
 10. Turn the lecture into notes. Transcribe the teacher’s voice, produce a short summary
 with the key points and likely questions, and let students ask questions that are answered
 only from that same lecture.
 11. Protect student privacy. Run the face analysis on the student’s own laptop, store no
 video or screen recording, tell students exactly what is collected, and keep a log of who
 viewed their information.
 12. Start simple, then prove the improvement. Get a basic version of each feature
 working first, improve it, and show with measured results that the improved version
 really is better.
 13. Test it properly. Evaluate with our own recorded samples, with up to 100 students
 connected at the same time, and with real teachers and students who use it and tell us
 what they think.

5. Domain of the Project
This is a development project in the domain of Artificial Intelligence–based web
application development for educational technology (EdTech). It draws on Computer
Vision, Machine Learning and Deep Learning, Natural Language Processing and Generative
AI, and joins them to real-time communication over WebRTC inside a containerised, service-
oriented cloud architecture.

6. Project Scope
What we will deliver is a working web-based classroom for live online lectures with: on-device
vision analysis (face presence, eye state and drowsiness, gaze and head direction); screen-to-
slide compliance checking; a confidence- and persistence-based alert engine; a private one-to-
one nudge from the instructor to a single student; an instructor dashboard with live alerts and
post-class analytics; lecture transcription with summaries and a grounded assistant; and a
containerised deployment evaluated with up to one hundred concurrent participants.
The following aspects are outside the scope of this project:
 • Examination proctoring or any automatic decision about cheating or misconduct. The
 system raises alerts only; every decision stays with the instructor.
 • Administrative classroom automation such as quiz generation, examination hosting,
 timetable or fee management. Attendance falls out of presence detection as a by-product
 and is not a module of this project.
 • Emotion recognition, and identity verification based on face recognition. Students are
 identified by their authenticated account and roll number.
 • Recording or storing raw video or continuous screen recordings. Only event metadata
 and the instructor’s lecture material are stored.
 • Operating-system level screen locking or hiding browser permission indicators, which a
 web application cannot do and should not try to.
 • Matching commercial meeting applications feature for feature (breakout rooms,
 whiteboards, cloud recording), or native mobile applications.
Assumptions: students attend on a laptop or desktop with a webcam and a current Chromium-
based browser; they consent explicitly to camera and screen sharing; the instructor uploads the
lecture slides (PDF/PPT) before class; the internet connection is good enough for a video call;
and lectures are delivered mainly in English, with some Urdu mixed in, which the speech-to-
text model supports.

7. Methodology
We follow an Agile incremental model with two-week sprints. Each sprint ends with a
working increment that is shown to the supervisor, so corrections arrive early instead of piling
up at the end. For every AI component we build the plain baseline first, measure it, and only
then replace or extend it with a learned model. That way each claimed improvement has a
number behind it.
7.1 Classroom Platform and User Management
 • Instructors and students register and sign in; JWT authentication and role-based access
 control guard every API endpoint.
 • Instructors create classes, enrol students by roll number and upload the slides that later
 act as the reference for screen compliance.
 • Before joining, each student sees a plain-language notice of which signals are analysed
 and grants camera, microphone and screen permissions one by one.
 • Audio and video travel over WebRTC through a Selective Forwarding Unit, which
 scales much better than peer-to-peer connections. Only the instructor publishes video; a
 student’s camera feeds the local vision worker and is never transmitted. Events and
 alerts use a separate secure WebSocket channel served by an in-process event bus, with
 Redis pub/sub added only if the API ever has to run as several replicas.
7.2 On-Device Vision Pipeline for Drowsiness and Attention
The first objective lives entirely in the student’s browser, built on MediaPipe Face Mesh
(Kartynnik et al., 2019) [13] running inside a Web Worker so that it keeps working when the
class tab is in the background. Camera frames are sampled at five to ten per second and 468
landmarks are extracted from each. From those we compute the Eye Aspect Ratio (Soukupová
& Čech, 2016) [10], blink rate and PERCLOS for drowsiness, and head pose together with gaze
direction for attention. Nothing but these numbers leaves the device. On the server, a temporal
classifier looks at a sliding window of them to decide the student’s state. A blink or a quick
glance is therefore not mistaken for drowsiness or distraction, and the classifier reports an
uncertain state whenever lighting or camera angle make the estimate unreliable. We will
compare the rule-based EAR baseline against a learned classifier trained on public datasets
such as the MRL Eye Dataset, YawDD and DAiSEE (Gupta et al., 2016) [7], together with a
small test set that we record ourselves, with consent.

7.3 Screen-Content Compliance and Slide Matching
The second objective asks whether what the student sees matches what is being taught. The
baseline needs no model at all: the browser reports whether the class tab is still visible and in
front, which already catches a switch to another window or application without taking a single
screenshot. The content comparison is the extension built on top of it. The shared screen is
sampled every few seconds, and a perceptual-hash filter drops frames that have not changed,
which removes most of the processing cost. Frames that did change go through OCR (Du et al.,
2020) [14] and image/text embeddings (Radford et al., 2021; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) [15],
[16], and the result is compared with the embedding of the instructor’s current slide and the
slides on either side of it. Scrolling, a page still loading, or opening a reference will all cause a
short mismatch, so a low similarity score has to persist beyond a configurable grace period
before a compliance event is raised.

7.4 Intelligent Alert Engine
The third objective is the part that turns raw detections into something an instructor can use.
Each event carries the student identifier, event type, timestamp, confidence, duration and the
module that produced it. An alert is raised only when three conditions hold at once: the
confidence is above a threshold, the condition has persisted longer than a configurable duration
(drowsiness beyond five seconds, say, or looking away beyond twenty), and the same alert is
not inside its cooldown window. Repeated events are grouped, which stops one struggling
student from flooding the dashboard, and alerts are ordered by severity. We will tune every
threshold on a validation set and report the values, rather than pick them by feel.
The engine also provides a private nudge. When the instructor taps an alert, a short message
and a soft sound go out over the WebSocket channel of that one student, who is quietly pulled
back to the lecture while the rest of the class hears and sees nothing. The nudge is addressed to a
single session identifier and never broadcast to the room; it is rate-limited, which keeps it from
being abused; and each one is written to the audit log with its sender, receiver and time. The
student sees who sent it and can answer with one tap, “I am back” or “I have a connection
problem”. That reply is stored as feedback on the alert and later used to measure how often the
alerts were justified.

7.5 Lecture Transcription and Learning Guidance
Lecture audio is transcribed close to real time with the open-source Whisper model (Radford et
al., 2023) [17] running through faster-whisper. The transcript is split by topic, and a small open-
source language model writes summaries, key points and likely examination questions. Slides
and transcript chunks are embedded and stored in a vector database. A retrieval-augmented
assistant (Lewis et al., 2020) [18] can then answer questions from that lecture’s material alone,
citing the slide it drew on and saying that a topic was not covered when the retrieved context
does not contain it.

7.8 Expected Research Contribution

 evaluated inside a live classroom instead of offline.

 screen content with instructor slides.
